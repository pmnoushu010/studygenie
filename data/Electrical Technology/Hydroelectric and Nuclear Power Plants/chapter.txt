PART I: HYDROELECTRIC POWER PLANTS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

1. INTRODUCTION & WORKING PRINCIPLE
A Hydroelectric Power Plant harnesses the gravitational potential energy of water stored at an elevated reservoir and converts it into electrical energy:
• Water stored behind a dam at a high elevation possesses potential energy (P.E. = m × g × H, where H is the head in meters).
• Water is channeled through high-pressure closed pipes called penstocks, where potential energy is converted into high-velocity kinetic energy.
• The high-velocity water jet strikes the runner vanes of a hydraulic turbine, converting hydraulic kinetic energy into mechanical rotational power at the turbine shaft.
• The hydraulic turbine is directly coupled to an AC synchronous generator (alternator), which converts rotational mechanical power into 3-phase alternating electrical energy.


2. HYDROLOGY & MASS CURVE
• Mass Curve (Rippl's Diagram):
  - A mass curve is a graphical plot of cumulative volume of river water inflow (where 1 second-metre month = 2592 × 10³ m³) against time (months of the year).
  - Slope of the curve at any point represents the rate of inflow (discharge) at that time.
  - Troughs represent periods of low water inflow (dry season/drought).
  - Peaks represent periods of heavy rainfall and flood runoff.
• Determining Reservoir Storage Capacity:
  - To determine the required storage capacity to maintain a uniform firm power yield:
    (i) Identify the trough points (e.g., points B and C) and draw a tangent line RS passing through the troughs. The slope of RS represents the uniform demand/draw-off rate.
    (ii) Draw a parallel tangent AX from the peak point (point A).
    (iii) The vertical distance measured between the upper tangent AX and the lower line RS represents the storage capacity required to ensure uninterrupted power generation during dry spells.


3. ESSENTIAL COMPONENTS OF A HYDROELECTRIC POWER PLANT
A complete hydroelectric installation comprises the following elements:
(i) Catchment Area:
    The surrounding mountainous geographical terrain from which all surface rainfall runoff drains into the river and dam basin.
(ii) Reservoir:
    A large natural or artificial body of water created by constructing a dam across the river to store water during monsoon periods for year-round utilization.
(iii) Dam & Head Works:
    - Built across the river to raise the water level and create the required hydraulic head (H).
    - Classified by materials:
      * Timber and Steel Dams: Low-head installations (6 m to 12 m).
      * Earth Dams: Built up to about 100 m on pervious rock/soil foundations. Consists of a central concrete core wall, earth/sand/gravel embankment, stone pitching at the water line against wave erosion, and turf/grass on exposed surfaces against rain erosion. Seepage loss is more than other dams.
      * Solid Gravity Dam: Heavy concrete or masonry structure that resists water overturning and sliding forces solely through its immense self-weight.
      * Concrete Buttress Dam: Has an inclined upstream face supported by triangular buttress walls; downward water pressure creates a large downward force providing inherent stability against sliding and overturning.
      * Arch Dam: Curved upstream masonry/concrete arch suitable for narrow canyon widths; transfers immense horizontal water thrust to canyon walls with less concrete.
(iv) Forebay:
    An enlarged body of water just above the intake (either a pond behind the diversion dam or an enlarged section of a canal) for storing water temporarily to meet hourly load fluctuations.
(v) Spillway:
    Acts as a safety valve. Discharges the overflow of water to the downstream side when the reservoir is full, mainly during flood periods.
    Classification: (a) Spillway dams or overflow spillways, (b) Chute spillways, (c) Side channel spillways, (d) Shaft spillways, and (e) Siphon spillways.
(vi) Penstock:
    A heavy-duty closed conduit (fabricated from high-tensile welded steel or reinforced concrete) connecting the forebay or surge tank to the scroll case of the hydraulic turbine.
(vii) Surge Tank:
    A small reservoir or vertical cylinder open at the top, installed on the penstock between the dam and power house.
    - Function & Water Hammer Protection:
      * When electrical load on the generator decreases, the governor tends to close turbine gates and water pressure is increased in the penstock. Water hammering occurs in the penstock if a surge tank is not provided. The water level in the surge tank rises, absorbing the momentum and relieving pressure variations.
      * Conversely, during larger load demand, the gate opens to allow large quantities of water to flow. There will be a tendency of vacuum or negative pressure in the water system; the surge tank provides immediate water to prevent penstock collapse.
(viii) Control Sluice Gates & Inlet Valves:
    Control and shut off water flow into the penstock and turbine runner.
(ix) Hydraulic Turbine (Hydrodynamic Machine):
    Converts fluid energy into rotational mechanical shaft torque.
(x) Tail Race:
    The discharge canal or channel leading spent water exiting the turbine draft tube back into the natural river.
(xi) Alternator, Switchgear & Power House:
    Houses the generator directly coupled to the turbine, step-up transformers, and circuit breakers to dispatch electrical energy to the transmission lines.


4. CLASSIFICATION OF HYDRODYNAMIC MACHINES (HYDRAULIC TURBINES)
The hydrodynamic machines, which are called hydraulic turbines, are classified as follows:
(a) According to the type of flow of water:
    (i) Axis along the shaft of machine — Axial flow turbines (Propeller turbine and Kaplan turbine).
    (ii) Axis along the radius — Radial flow turbines (Francis turbine).
    (iii) Axis along the tangential directions — Tangential flow turbines (Pelton wheel).
    (iv) Mixed flow (Radial inlet and axial outlet) — Francis turbine.
(b) According to action of water:
    (i) Impulse Turbine: Total hydraulic head is converted into kinetic energy in nozzles before entering runner at atmospheric pressure (e.g., Pelton Wheel).
    (ii) Reaction Turbine: Water enters runner under pressure; both pressure energy and kinetic energy drive the runner inside a fully enclosed casing (e.g., Francis and Kaplan Turbines).


5. MERITS AND DEMERITS OF HYDROPOWER PLANTS
Merits:
(a) No fuel is required by a hydropower station.
(b) It is a very neat and clean plant.
(c) Operation and maintenance costs are very cheap.
(d) Load can be varied quickly and rapidly.
(e) The life of the plant is long (50 to 100+ years) and the efficiency of the plant does not decrease with age.
(f) During operation and maintenance, highly skilled engineers are not required.
(g) There are no standby losses in the plant.
(h) By the acute governing system, constant speed and frequency can be maintained.
(i) The plant can be run up and synchronised very quickly.
(j) This plant can help in irrigation and flood control.

Demerits:
(a) Construction cost is very high and installation time is very long.
(b) Long transmission lines are required because it is at the remote end.
(c) Output of the plant is not constant because it totally depends on monsoon.
(d) It needs a large area.
(e) Sometimes during flood time, excess water is to be drained out, creating problems for nearby inhabitants.

PART II: NUCLEAR POWER PLANTS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

1. INTRODUCTION & WORKING PRINCIPLE
The purpose of a nuclear reactor is to generate heat. The nuclear heat is produced by splitting of the nucleus (nuclear fission).
Fundamental Constituents of a Nuclear Power Plant:
(a) Nuclear reactor (generates heat through controlled nuclear fission).
(b) Heat exchanger / Steam generator (transfers heat from hot reactor coolant to feed water to produce steam).
(c) Steam turbine (converts enthalpy of steam into shaft rotary mechanical power).
(d) Condenser (condenses exhaust steam back into pure water).
(e) Alternator (converts shaft mechanical power into electrical energy).


2. NUCLEAR FISSION REACTION & ENERGY DENSITY
• Fission Process:
  - Fission occurs when the nucleus of a heavy radioactive atom (such as U²³⁵, Th²³², or Pu²³⁹) is bombarded by a thermal neutron and splits into two elements of lower mass number (such as krypton and barium), releasing 2 to 3 fast neutrons and an immense amount of energy (~200 MeV per fission).
  - Energy Equivalent:
    * 1 eV = 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ J;  200 MeV = 3.2 × 10⁻¹¹ J per fission event.
    * The splitting of 1 kg of uranium atoms yields 25 × 10⁶ kWh in heat form.
    * The energy obtained by fission of 1 kg of uranium is approximately equivalent to burning 2,500 tonnes of coal.
    * About 6.5 × 10⁶ kWh electrical energy is developed from 1 kg of uranium.
  - Chain Reaction:
    * Fast neutrons are ejected at a high speed of 2 × 10⁷ m/s.
    * The released two or three neutrons may cause fission reactions with other U²³⁵ nuclei.
    * When such a reaction is controlled, a steady chain reaction occurs. If not controlled, an explosive release of energy (atom bomb) may occur.


3. SEVEN CORE COMPONENTS OF A NUCLEAR REACTOR
A nuclear reactor consists of the following seven components:
(a) Fuel:
    - The reactor consists of U²³⁸₉₂, Th²³²₉₀, etc. as nuclear fuel.
    - Atomic number of uranium is 92 and atomic mass is 238. Atomic number of thorium is 90 and atomic mass is 232.
    - Natural uranium consists of:
      * 99.23% of U²³⁸₉₂
      * 0.71% of U²³⁵₉₂
      * 0.06% of U²³⁴₉₂
    - Enriched with U²³⁵₉₂ along with aluminium or zirconium for dilution.
(b) Moderator:
    - Objective: To reduce fast neutron speed (from 2 × 10⁷ m/s down to thermal velocities). When fast neutrons collide with nuclei of moderator material of equal mass, they lose energy through elastic collisions.
    - Desirable properties:
      (i) High scattering cross-section.
      (ii) Low neutron absorption cross-section.
    - Materials: Hydrogen, deuterium, helium, lithium, beryllium, boron, carbon (graphite), nitrogen, and oxygen. Heavy water (D₂O) is an ideal moderating material.
(c) Reflector:
    - Surrounds the reactor core within the thermal shielding arrangement.
    - Bounces back escaping neutrons into the core, reducing their speed to sustain the chain reaction. Must have good scattering and low absorption properties.
(d) Control Rods:
    - Regulate fissioning in the reactor by absorbing excess neutrons.
    - Made of Boron (or Cadmium) and inserted into the core from the top of the reactor vessel.
    - Moved out slightly to increase fissioning rate; moved in to absorb more neutrons and reduce power or shut down.
(e) Coolant:
    - Medium through which heat developed in the reactor is transferred to the heat exchanger for power generation:
      (i) Gases: Air, helium, hydrogen, and CO₂.
      (ii) Liquids: Light water (H₂O) and heavy water (D₂O).
      (iii) Liquid Metals: Molten sodium and lithium.
(f) Thermal and Biological Shielding:
    - Surrounds the entire reactor core. Absorbs radiations in the form of α, β particles, γ rays and escaping neutrons developed by fissioning.
    - Inner shield: Constructed from iron, cooled by circulating coolant.
    - Outer shield: High-density concrete biological shield (2 to 3 meters thick) to protect personnel.
(g) Structure & Reactor Vessel:
    - High-strength pressure vessel and containment structure housing the reactor core components.


4. MERITS AND DEMERITS OF NUCLEAR POWER PLANTS
Merits:
(a) The amount of fuel required is quite small; hence there is no problem of transportation.
(b) The plants require less area as compared to any other type of the same size.
(c) It not only produces large amounts of power, but also produces valuable fissile material (plutonium) extracted when fuel is renewed.
(d) Can be located very near the load centre; primary power distribution cost can be reduced.
(e) Most economical in case of large capacity.
(f) Large amount of nuclear fuels in the world; nuclear plants can be run for thousands of years.
(g) The control of the output is extremely flexible.

Demerits:
(a) The initial capital cost is very high.
(b) Technical knowledge is required for erection and commissioning.
(c) There may occur a dangerous amount of radioactive pollution.
(d) It is not well suited for varying loads.
(e) The fuel is expensive and is difficult to recover.
(f) Maintenance charge is high since large numbers of specially trained personnel are required.
(g) The disposal of radioactive products is a big problem.


5. SELECTION OF SITE OF NUCLEAR POWER PLANTS
(a) Proximity to Water Body: Site selected should be near a river, lake, or seaside for condenser cooling water.
(b) Safety Distance: Reasonable distance between the plant and the nearest populated area to mitigate atmospheric radioactivity hazard.
(c) Transportation Facilities: Required during the construction stage for handling heavy machinery and reactor vessels.
(d) Proximity to Load Centre: Plants should be located as near to the load centre as possible.
(e) Radioactive Waste Disposal: Site should have adequate space for disposal of radioactive waste.
(f) Soil Bearing Capacity: The land should have the supporting strength to carry reactors having a weight of 1,00,000 tonnes and to impose a bearing pressure of 50 tonnes/m².
