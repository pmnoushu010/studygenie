PART I: HYDROELECTRIC POWER PLANTS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

1. INTRODUCTION & WORKING PRINCIPLE
A Hydroelectric Power Plant harnesses the gravitational potential energy of water stored at an elevated reservoir and converts it into electrical energy:
• Water stored behind a dam at a high elevation possesses potential energy (P.E. = m * g * H, where H is the head in meters).
• Water is channeled through high-pressure closed pipes called penstocks, where potential energy is converted into high-velocity kinetic energy.
• The high-velocity water jet strikes the runner vanes of a hydraulic turbine, converting hydraulic kinetic energy into mechanical rotational power at the turbine shaft.
• The hydraulic turbine is directly coupled to an AC synchronous generator (alternator), which converts rotational mechanical power into 3-phase alternating electrical energy.


2. HYDROLOGY & MASS CURVE
• Mass Curve (Rippl's Diagram):
  - A mass curve is a graphical plot of cumulative volume of river water inflow (expressed in second-metre-months or million cubic meters) against time (months of the year).
  - Slope of the curve at any point represents the rate of inflow (discharge) at that time.
  - Troughs represent periods of low water inflow (dry season/drought).
  - Peaks represent periods of heavy rainfall and flood runoff.
• Determining Reservoir Storage Capacity:
  - To determine the required storage capacity to maintain a uniform firm power yield:
    1. Identify the trough points (e.g., points B and C) and draw a tangent line RS passing through the troughs. The slope of RS represents the uniform demand/draw-off rate.
    2. Draw a parallel tangent AX from the peak point (point A).
    3. The maximum vertical distance measured between the upper tangent AX and the lower line RS represents the minimum reservoir storage capacity required to ensure uninterrupted power generation during dry spells.


3. ESSENTIAL COMPONENTS OF A HYDROELECTRIC POWER PLANT
A complete hydroelectric installation comprises the following elements:
1. Catchment Area:
   The surrounding mountainous geographical terrain from which all surface rainfall runoff drains into the river and dam basin.
2. Reservoir:
   A large natural or artificial body of water created by constructing a dam across the river to store water during monsoon periods for year-round utilization.
3. Dam & Head Works:
   - Built across the river to raise the water level and create the required hydraulic head (H).
   - Classified by materials:
     * Timber and Steel Dams: Low-head installations (6 m to 12 m).
     * Earth Dams: Built up to 100 m on pervious rock/soil foundations. Consists of an impermeable concrete core wall, earth/sand/gravel embankment, stone pitching on the upstream face against wave erosion, and turf/grass on the downstream face against rain erosion. Subject to higher seepage loss.
     * Solid Gravity Dam: Heavy concrete or masonry structure that resists water overturning and sliding forces solely through its immense self-weight.
     * Concrete Buttress Dam: Has an inclined upstream face supported by triangular buttress walls; downward water pressure provides inherent stability against sliding and overturning.
     * Arch Dam: Curved upstream masonry/concrete arch suitable for narrow, rocky gorges; transfers immense horizontal water thrust to the canyon walls.
4. Forebay:
   An enlarged regulating water basin located just upstream of the penstock intake to temporarily store water and buffer hourly load fluctuations on the turbines.
5. Spillway:
   Acts as a safety valve for the dam. When the reservoir is full during severe floods, the spillway safely discharges excess flood water downstream to prevent overtopping and dam failure.
   Types: Overflow/Ogee spillways, Chute spillways, Side channel spillways, Shaft (morning glory) spillways, and Siphon spillways.
6. Penstock:
   A heavy-duty closed conduit (fabricated from high-tensile welded steel or prestressed concrete) carrying high-pressure water from the forebay or surge tank down to the turbine spiral casing.
7. Surge Tank:
   An open-topped vertical cylinder or auxiliary tank installed on long penstocks between the reservoir and the power house.
   - Purpose & Water Hammer Protection:
     * When electrical load on the generator suddenly drops, the governor rapidly closes the turbine inlet gates. The sudden deceleration of high-momentum water causes a massive pressure surge called "water hammer", which can rupture penstock pipes.
     * The surge tank absorbs the rejected water, allowing the water level inside it to rise, converting kinetic energy into potential energy and neutralizing the pressure wave.
     * Conversely, during sudden load increase, the gates open quickly. The surge tank provides immediate extra water, preventing vacuum/negative pressure that could cause thin penstock walls to buckle inward.
8. Control Gates & Inlet Valves:
   Sluice gates at the dam intake and rotary/butterfly valves at the turbine inlet control and shut off water flow during emergency maintenance.
9. Hydraulic Turbine:
   Converts the fluid energy into mechanical rotary power.
10. Tail Race:
    The discharge canal or channel that leads spent water exiting the turbine draft tube back into the natural river bed.
11. Power House & Switchgear:
    Houses the hydraulic turbines, alternators, exciters, step-up transformers, circuit breakers, and protection relays.


4. CLASSIFICATION OF HYDRAULIC TURBINES (HYDRODYNAMIC MACHINES)
1. According to Flow Direction across Runner:
   • Tangential Flow Turbine: Water jet strikes runner buckets along the tangent (e.g., Pelton Wheel). High head (150 m to 1500 m), low discharge.
   • Radial Flow Turbine: Water flows inward along the radius towards the shaft center (e.g., Old Francis Turbine).
   • Axial Flow Turbine: Water flows parallel to the turbine shaft axis (e.g., Propeller and Kaplan Turbines with adjustable runner blades). Low head (< 30 m), large discharge.
   • Mixed Flow Turbine: Water enters radially inward and leaves axially downward (e.g., Modern Francis Turbine). Medium head (30 m to 300 m).
2. According to Action of Water:
   • Impulse Turbine: Total hydraulic head is converted into kinetic energy in nozzles before entering runner at atmospheric pressure (e.g., Pelton Wheel).
   • Reaction Turbine: Water enters runner under pressure; both pressure energy and kinetic energy drive the runner inside a fully enclosed casing (e.g., Francis and Kaplan Turbines).


5. MERITS AND DEMERITS OF HYDROPOWER PLANTS
Merits:
• Zero Fuel Cost: Utilizes naturally flowing water; zero recurring fuel expenses.
• Environmentally Clean: Zero combustion emissions, greenhouse gases, or toxic ash.
• Low Operating & Maintenance Costs: Simple mechanical equipment with minimal wear.
• High Reliability & Rapid Load Response: Highly flexible; synchronizes and picks up full load within minutes; ideal for peak load and frequency regulation.
• Extremely Long Plant Life: Typically 50 to 100+ years without efficiency degradation.
• Multi-Purpose Benefits: Dam construction aids irrigation, drinking water supply, and downstream flood control.
• No Standby Losses: Consumes zero energy when shut down on standby.

Demerits:
• High Initial Capital Cost: Massive civil investments in dams, tunnels, and penstocks.
• Long Gestation Period: Construction and commissioning take 5 to 10 years.
• Seasonal Dependence: Plant generation is heavily dependent on annual rainfall and monsoon cycles.
• Remote Geographical Locations: Built in distant hilly areas, requiring long and expensive high-voltage transmission lines to load centers.
• Environmental & Submersion Issues: Submerges vast forests and agricultural lands, displacing communities and causing ecological disruption.

PART II: NUCLEAR POWER PLANTS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

1. INTRODUCTION & WORKING PRINCIPLE
A Nuclear Power Plant utilizes the immense thermal energy released during nuclear fission reactions to generate high-pressure steam, which expands in a conventional steam turbine coupled to an alternator to produce electricity.

Fundamental Constituents:
(a) Nuclear Reactor (generates heat through controlled fission).
(b) Heat Exchanger / Steam Generator (transfers reactor heat to secondary water to produce steam).
(c) Steam Turbine (converts thermal enthalpy of steam into shaft rotary mechanical power).
(d) Surface Condenser (condenses exhaust steam back into pure water for boiler recirculation).
(e) Synchronous Alternator (converts shaft mechanical power into 3-phase electrical energy).


2. NUCLEAR FISSION REACTION & ENERGY DENSITY
• Fission Process:
  - When the nucleus of a heavy fissile isotope (such as Uranium-235, Plutonium-239, or Thorium-232) is struck by a slow (thermal) neutron, it splits into two lighter fission fragments (e.g., Barium-141 and Krypton-92) accompanied by the ejection of 2 to 3 fast neutrons and an enormous release of energy.
  - Nuclear Equation:
    U-235 + n_thermal -> Ba-141 + Kr-92 + 3 n_fast + 200 MeV Energy
  - Energy Equivalent:
    * 1 eV = 1.6 * 10^-19 Joules; 200 MeV = 3.2 * 10^-11 Joules per fission event.
    * 1 kg of Uranium-235 undergoing complete fission releases approximately 25 * 10^6 kWh of heat energy, equivalent to burning 2,500 tonnes of high-grade coal.
    * Developing ~6.5 * 10^6 kWh of electrical energy from 1 kg of uranium.
  - Chain Reaction:
    * If the released fast neutrons are slowed down and absorbed by surrounding U-235 nuclei, a self-sustaining chain reaction occurs.
    * In a power reactor, this chain reaction is strictly controlled so that exactly one neutron from each fission causes another fission (Multiplication factor k = 1). Uncontrolled multiplication causes an explosive release of energy.


3. SEVEN CORE COMPONENTS OF A NUCLEAR REACTOR
1. Nuclear Fuel:
   - Contains fissile material: Natural Uranium (99.23% U-238, 0.71% U-235, 0.06% U-234), Low-Enriched Uranium (2% to 5% U-235), Thorium-232, or Plutonium-239.
   - Formed into ceramic uranium dioxide (UO2) pellets encapsulated in corrosion-resistant zirconium alloy (Zircaloy) or aluminum tubes (fuel rods).
2. Moderator:
   - Fast neutrons are emitted at velocities around 2 * 10^7 m/s (kinetic energy ~2 MeV). U-235 fission occurs most readily with slow "thermal" neutrons (~2,200 m/s, energy ~0.025 eV).
   - The moderator slows down fast neutrons through repeated elastic collisions without absorbing them.
   - Ideal Properties: High scattering cross-section and low neutron absorption cross-section.
   - Materials Used: Heavy water (D2O - the most effective moderator), high-purity reactor-grade Graphite (carbon), Light water (ordinary H2O), and Beryllium.
3. Reflector:
   - Surrounds the reactor core to bounce escaping neutrons back into the fuel core, minimizing neutron leakage, conserving fuel, and reducing the critical size of the reactor.
   - Made of materials with high scattering and low absorption properties (Graphite, Beryllium, or Heavy Water).
4. Control Rods:
   - Regulate the neutron multiplication rate and thermal power output, or shut down the reactor instantly during an emergency (SCRAM).
   - Made of elements that have very high neutron absorption cross-sections (Boron, Cadmium, or Hafnium).
   - Inserted deeper into the core to absorb more neutrons and decrease power; withdrawn slightly to increase power.
5. Coolant:
   - Circulates continuously through the reactor core to remove the intense heat generated by nuclear fission and transfer it to the heat exchanger/steam generator.
   - Coolant Materials:
     * Gases: Carbon dioxide (CO2), Helium (He), Nitrogen, Air.
     * Liquids: Demineralized light water (H2O), Heavy water (D2O).
     * Liquid Metals: Molten Sodium (Na), Sodium-Potassium alloy (NaK), Lithium.
6. Thermal and Biological Shielding:
   - Protects operating personnel and surroundings from harmful ionizing radiations (alpha, beta, gamma rays) and escaping neutrons.
   - Inner Thermal Shield: Heavy iron/steel lining absorbing intense radiation and heat.
   - Outer Biological Shield: Thick high-density concrete walls (2 to 3 meters thick) surrounding the entire reactor vessel.
7. Reactor Vessel & Containment Structure:
   - High-strength pressure vessel housing the core, submerged in coolant, surrounded by a reinforced concrete containment dome capable of withstanding extreme internal pressures in emergencies.


4. MERITS AND DEMERITS OF NUCLEAR POWER PLANTS
Merits:
• Negligible Fuel Transportation Burden: Tiny fuel volume eliminates bulk rail/road transportation logistics required for coal.
• Compact Land Requirement: Occupies far less ground area than hydro or solar installations of equivalent capacity.
• Breeds Nuclear Fuel: Breeder reactors convert fertile U-238 and Th-232 into fissile Pu-239 and U-233, creating additional valuable fuel.
• Near Load Centers: Can be built near industrial load centers, substantially lowering transmission line losses and costs.
• Highly Economical at Large Capacities: Very low fuel cost per kWh in large baseload stations (> 1000 MW).
• Vast Global Reserves: Nuclear fuel reserves can supply world energy needs for centuries.

Demerits:
• High Initial Capital Cost: Extremely expensive reactor components, safety systems, and containment civil works.
• Specialized Technical Expertise: Demands highly trained nuclear engineers and technicians for operation, maintenance, and radiological safety.
• Radiation Hazard: Catastrophic contamination risks in the event of severe accidents or containment breach.
• Inflexible for Variable Loads: Designed to run continuously at steady baseload; poorly suited for rapid load-following.
• Radioactive Waste Disposal: Spent fuel and radioactive fission products remain hazardously radioactive for thousands of years, requiring deep underground repositories.


5. SELECTION OF SITE FOR A NUCLEAR POWER PLANT
1. Proximity to Abundant Water Body: Must be located adjacent to a river, lake, or seashore to ensure vast supplies of cooling water for steam condensers.
2. Distance from Populated Areas: Must maintain an exclusion zone and sterilized boundary from towns and cities to minimize radiological exposure risks.
3. Excellent Transportation Access: Heavy reactor components (vessels weighing hundreds of tonnes) require heavy rail, highway, or marine transport access.
4. Heavy Foundation Bearing Capacity: The land must have exceptional geological bearing strength (> 50 tonnes/m²) on bedrock to support massive reactor vessels and biological shields exceeding 100,000 tonnes.
5. Seismic Stability: Located far from active earthquake fault lines or tsunami zones.
6. Adequate Space for Radioactive Waste Storage: Dedicated, secure facilities for spent fuel pool storage and intermediate radwaste management.
