PART I: THREE-PHASE INDUCTION MOTOR
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

1. INTRODUCTION & WORKING PRINCIPLE
A three-phase induction motor is also known as a rotating transformer which works on AC:
• Stator winding acts like the primary winding connected to a 3-phase AC supply.
• Rotor winding acts like a short-circuited secondary winding.
• Energy is transferred across the air-gap through mutual electromagnetic induction without any conductive connection.

Working Principle:
(i) When stator winding is connected to 3-phase AC supply, a rotating magnetic field of constant magnitude is produced revolving at synchronous speed:
    Nₛ = 120 f / P  (rpm)
(ii) It cuts rotor conductors and an EMF is induced due to relative speed of rotating magnetic field and rotor conductors.
(iii) Since the rotor circuit is closed by end rings, circulating current I₂ flows in the circuit.
(iv) The current-carrying conductor is placed in magnetic field; force is produced on all conductors (F = B I l) which in turn produce torque T.
(v) It moves in the same direction of magnetic field in order to reduce relative speed between them by Lenz's law.


2. CONSTRUCTIONAL DETAILS
It mainly consists of stator and rotor:

A. Stator:
• The stator of an induction motor is a laminated iron core with slots.
• Coils are placed in the slots to form a balanced three-phase distributed winding.

B. Rotor:
Two types of rotor:
(i) Squirrel Cage Rotor:
    • Rotor is composed of copper/aluminum bars embedded in the rotor slots and shorted at both ends by end rings.
    • It resembles a SQUIRREL CAGE. The motor with squirrel cage rotor is known as a squirrel cage induction motor.
    • Simple, low cost, robust, low maintenance.
    • Bars are slightly skewed to prevent cogging and reduce noise.
(ii) Slip Ring Induction Motor / Wound Rotor:
    • Rotor winding is wound by insulated wires. The winding terminals can be connected to external circuits through slip rings and brushes.
    • The three slip rings are short-circuited through an external resistance.
    • External resistance is inserted to obtain high starting torque.
    • Easy to control speed, but more expensive and requires brush maintenance.


3. COMPARISON OF SQUIRREL CAGE AND SLIP RING INDUCTION MOTOR
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Parameter               Squirrel Cage Induction Motor   Slip Ring Induction Motor
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
1. Starting Torque      Low Starting torque             High starting torque
2. Copper Losses & Eff. Copper losses are less and      Copper losses are high and
                        efficiency is large             efficiency is low
3. Cost                 Low cost                        High cost

                                                        be reduced using external resistances
5. Maintenance          Requires low maintenance        Requires high maintenance

4. MATHEMATICAL RELATIONS & ROTOR QUANTITIES
• Synchronous Speed (Nₛ): Speed of rotating magnetic field:
  Nₛ = 120 f / P  (rpm)
• Slip (s): Difference between synchronous speed (Nₛ) and actual rotor speed (N):
  s = (Nₛ - N) / Nₛ
• Rotor Speed (N): Speed of motor:
  N = Nₛ (1 - s)
• Rotor Current Frequency (f'):
  For a rotor speed N, the relative speed between the rotating flux and the rotor is Nₛ - N.
  Consequently, the rotor current frequency f' is given by:
  f' = [(Nₛ - N) P] / 120 = (s Nₛ P) / 120 = s f
  (Since s = (Nₛ - N) / Nₛ  and  f = Nₛ P / 120).

• Rotor EMF:
  (i) At Standstill: The motor is equivalent to a three-phase transformer with secondary short-circuited:
      E₂ = E₁ × (N₂ / N₁)
      where N₁ = number of turns per phase on stator, N₂ = number of turns per phase on rotor.
  (ii) Running Condition: When the rotor starts running, the EMF induced in rotor is given by sE₂.

• Rotor Circuit Parameters:
  Let the resistance and inductance per phase of rotor be R₂ and L₂ respectively, and induced EMF at standstill be E₂.
  (i) At Standstill:
      - Induced EMF / phase = E₂
      - Rotor winding resistance / phase = R₂
      - Rotor winding reactance / phase: X₂ = 2π f L₂  (where f is supply frequency)
      - Rotor winding impedance / phase: Z₂ = √(R₂² + X₂²)
      - Standstill rotor current: I₂ = E₂ / Z₂ = E₂ / √(R₂² + X₂²)
      - Standstill power factor: cos ϕ₂ = R₂ / √(R₂² + X₂²)
  (ii) At Slip s:
      - Induced EMF in rotor winding = sE₂
      - Rotor winding resistance / phase = R₂
      - Rotor winding reactance / phase: X₂' = 2π f' L₂ = 2π s f L₂ = s X₂
      - Rotor winding impedance / phase: Z₂' = √[R₂² + (s X₂)²]
      - Rotor current per phase: I₂ = (s E₂) / √[R₂² + (s X₂)²]
      - Power factor of rotor current: cos ϕ₂ = R₂ / √[R₂² + (s X₂)²]


5. TORQUE OF AN INDUCTION MOTOR
Torque of an induction motor is due to interaction of rotor and stator fields and phase relation between them.
Torque developed by rotor of an induction motor is directly proportional to:
(i) rotor current I₂
(ii) stator flux per pole Φ
(iii) power factor of rotor circuit cos ϕ₂

Therefore:
T ∝ Φ I₂ cos ϕ₂
We know that rotor EMF E₂ ∝ Φ.
Therefore:
T ∝ E₂ I₂ cos ϕ₂   ⇒   T = K E₂ I₂ cos ϕ₂

Substituting the values of I₂ and cos ϕ₂:
T = K E₂ × [(s E₂) / √[R₂² + (s X₂)²]] × [R₂ / √[R₂² + (s X₂)²]]
T = (K s R₂ E₂²) / (R₂² + s² X₂²)

• Starting Torque (T_st):
  At starting, s = 1:
  T_st = (K R₂ E₂²) / (R₂² + X₂²)

• Condition for Maximum Running Torque:
  T = (K₂ s R₂) / (R₂² + s² X₂²)
  In order to find the value of rotor resistance that gives maximum torque under running conditions, differentiate T w.r.t. s and equate the result to zero (dT/ds = 0):
  dT / ds = [K₂ R₂ (R₂² + s² X₂²) - 2s X₂² (s R₂)] / (R₂² + s² X₂²)² = 0
  (R₂² + s² X₂²) - 2s² X₂² = 0
  R₂² = s² X₂²   ⇒   R₂ = s X₂
  Thus for maximum torque (T_max) under running conditions:
  Rotor resistance / phase = Fractional slip × Standstill rotor reactance / phase   (s_m = R₂ / X₂).
  Substituting value of R₂ in equation yields:
  T_max = (K E₂²) / (2 X₂)

• Torque-Slip Characteristics:
  (i) At s = 0, T = 0 so that torque-slip curve starts from the origin.
  (ii) At normal speed, slip is small so that s X₂ is negligible as compared to R₂:
       T ∝ s / R₂ ∝ s  (as R₂ is constant)
       Hence torque slip curve is a straight line from zero slip to full-load slip.
  (iii) As slip increases beyond full-load slip, the torque increases and becomes maximum at s = R₂ / X₂. This maximum torque is called pull-out torque or break-down torque. Its value is at least twice the full-load value.
  (iv) Beyond maximum torque, the term s² X₂² increases very rapidly so that R₂² may be neglected:
       T ∝ s / (s² X₂²) ∝ 1 / s  (as X₂ is constant)
       Thus torque is now inversely proportional to slip; the curve is a rectangular hyperbola.
  (v) The maximum torque remains the same and is independent of the value of rotor resistance. Addition of external resistance to rotor circuit changes the value of slip at which maximum torque occurs, substantially increasing starting torque.


6. POWER FLOW DIAGRAM
From the power flow diagram:
• Stator Input: P_i
• Stator Cu & Iron Loss
• Rotor Power Input: P_ir = 2π Nₛ T_d / 60
• Rotor Cu Loss: W_cu = P_ir - P_d = s P_ir
• Mechanical Power Developed: P_d = 2π N T_d / 60 = (1 - s) P_ir
• Golden Power Ratio:
  P_ir : W_cu : P_d = 1 : s : (1 - s)
• Mechanical losses (friction and windage loss T_f)
• Net Shaft Output Power: P_out = 2π N T_sh / 60


7. STARTING OF INDUCTION MOTOR
Starting methods:
(i) Direct-on-line starting:
    • Motor is started by connecting it directly to 3-phase supply.
    • Standstill impedance is relatively low; starting current is high (4 to 10 times full-load current) at low power factor.
    • Suitable for relatively small (up to 7.5 kW) machines.
(ii) Stator resistance starting:
    • External resistances are connected in series with each phase of stator winding during starting.
    • Causes voltage drop across resistances so voltage available across motor terminals is reduced, limiting starting current.
    • Starting resistances are gradually cut out in steps as motor picks up speed.
(iii) Autotransformer starting:
    • Connects induction motor to reduced voltage tap (50% to 80%) at starting, then connects to full voltage as motor picks up ~80% speed.
    • Reduces starting current to safe limits.
(iv) Star-delta starting:
    • Stator winding designed for delta operation is connected in star during starting.
    • Each stator phase gets V / √3 volts (where V is line voltage).
    • Starting current and starting torque are reduced to 1/3 of direct delta values.
(v) Rotor resistance starting:
    • Applicable only to slip-ring motors.
    • A variable star-connected rheostat is connected in the rotor circuit through slip rings.
    • (i) At starting, rheostat handle is set in OFF position so maximum resistance is in each phase, reducing starting current and increasing starting torque.
    • (ii) As motor picks up speed, rheostat handle is gradually moved clockwise, cutting out external resistance until normal running speed is reached.
Note: Methods (i) to (iv) are applicable to both squirrel-cage and slip-ring motors; method (v) is applicable only to slip-ring motors.


8. SPEED CONTROL OF INDUCTION MOTOR
Speed of the induction motor is given by:
N_r = Nₛ (1 - s) = [120 f / P] (1 - s)
Speed of induction motor can be changed by:
(i) By changing frequency (f)
(ii) By changing number of poles (P)
(iii) By changing slip (s)

PART II: SYNCHRONOUS MACHINE (ALTERNATORS & SYNCHRONOUS MOTORS)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

1. CONSTRUCTION & ADVANTAGES OF STATIONARY ARMATURE
Construction:
• Stator: Stationary 3-phase armature winding housed in a laminated core (special steel stampings insulated with varnish to reduce eddy current loss; steel material reduces hysteresis loss).
• Advantages of Stationary Armature:
  (i) It is easier to insulate stationary winding for high voltages for which alternators are usually designed, because they are not subjected to centrifugal forces and extra slot space is available.
  (ii) The stationary 3-phase armature can be directly connected to load without going through large, unreliable slip rings and brushes.
  (iii) Only two slip rings are required for DC supply to the field winding on the rotor; exciting current is small, so slip rings and brush gear are of light construction.
  (iv) Due to simple and robust construction of the rotor, higher speed of rotating DC field is possible, increasing output obtainable from a machine of given dimensions.


2. ROTOR CONSTRUCTION
(i) Salient Pole Type:
    • Projected pole type as all poles are projected out from the rotor surface.
    • Large diameter and small axial length.
    • Mechanical strength is less; preferred for low speed alternators (125 rpm to 500 rpm).
    • Applications: Water turbines, I.C. engines.
(ii) Smooth Cylindrical or Non-Salient Pole:
    • Smooth solid steel cylinder having slots to accommodate distributed field coils.
    • Small diameter and large axial length.
    • Very high mechanical strength; preferred for high speed turbo-alternators (1500 rpm to 3000 rpm).
    • Applications: Steam turbines, electric motors.


3. BASIC TERMS & EMF EQUATION OF ALTERNATOR
Basic Terms:
(i) Turn (T): Consists of two conductors connected to one end by an end connector.
(ii) Coil: Formed by connecting several turns in series.
(ii)(a) Conductor (Z): Active length of wire present in field. In a multiturn coil, conductors Z = 2T.
(iii) Winding: Formed by connecting several coils in series.
• Electrical and Mechanical Degree:
  For a P pole machine: θ_ed = (P / 2) θ_md
• Pole Pitch: Angular distance between centers of two adjacent poles = 180° electrical.
• Coil Pitch: Distance between two sides of a coil. Full pitch coil if coil pitch = pole pitch = 180° electrical. Short pitch coil if coil span < 180° electrical.

Winding Factors:
(i) Pitch Factor / Coil Span Factor (Kₚ):
    Kₚ = cos(α / 2)   (where α = short-pitch angle; Kₚ = 1 for full-pitch, Kₚ < 1 for short-pitch).
(ii) Distribution Factor / Breadth Factor (K_d):
    Coils distributed in slots per pole per phase (m = slots / pole / phase, slot angle β = 180° / (slots/pole)):
    K_d = sin(mβ / 2) / [m sin(β / 2)]   (Value of K_d is less than 1).
• Winding Factor: K_w = Kₚ × K_d.

EMF Equation of Alternator:
Let Z = No. of conductors in series per phase, Φ = Flux per pole in Webers, P = No. of rotor poles, N = Rotor speed in rpm.
In one revolution (60 / N second), each stator conductor is cut by P Φ Webers:
• Average induced EMF per conductor = dΦ / dt = (P Φ) / (60 / N) = (P Φ N) / 60  volts.
  Since N = 120 f / P, Average EMF per conductor = 2 f Φ  volts.
• Average EMF / phase = 2 f Φ Z  volts.
• R.M.S. value of EMF / phase = Average value / phase × Form Factor (1.11):
  E_{r.m.s.} / phase = 2.22 f Φ Z  volts.
  Since Z = 2T:
  E_{r.m.s.} / phase = 4.44 f Φ T  volts.
• Accounting for pitch factor Kₚ and distribution factor K_d:
  E_{r.m.s.} / phase = 2.22 Kₚ K_d f Φ Z  volts
  E_{r.m.s.} / phase = 4.44 Kₚ K_d f Φ T  volts.


4. VOLTAGE REGULATION OF ALTERNATOR
Voltage regulation of an alternator is defined as the increase in terminal voltage expressed as percentage of full-load terminal voltage, when load at a given power factor is thrown off, with speed and field current remaining same:
% Voltage regulation = [(E₀ - V) / V] × 100

Factors affecting voltage regulation:
(i) Iₐ Rₐ drop in armature winding
(ii) Iₐ X_L drop in armature winding
(iii) Voltage change due to armature reaction

Synchronous Impedance / EMF Method:
(i) Armature resistance Rₐ per phase: Measured by DC voltmeter-ammeter method; effective AC resistance Rₐ = 1.5 R_dc.
(ii) Open-circuit characteristic (O.C.C.): Armature terminal phase voltage on open circuit vs. field current I_f at rated speed.
(iii) Short-circuit characteristic (S.C.C.): Short-circuit armature current I_sc vs. field current I_f.
From tests:
Zₛ = E_rated(open circuit) / I_sc(short circuit test)
Xₛ = √(Zₛ² - Rₐ²)

Phasor Equation:
E₀ = √[(V cos ϕ + Iₐ Rₐ)² + (V sin ϕ ± Iₐ Xₛ)²]
(+ sign for lagging power factor, - sign for leading power factor, ϕ = 0 for UPF).


5. SYNCHRONOUS MOTOR & SYNCHRONOUS CONDENSER
Construction & Features:
A synchronous motor operates at synchronous speed (N = Nₛ = 120 f / P) and converts electrical energy into mechanical energy.
Salient features:
(i) A synchronous motor runs at synchronous speed or not at all at all loads.
(ii) Outstanding characteristic: can be made to operate over a wide range of power factors (lagging, unity, or leading) by adjustment of field excitation.
(iii) Generally of salient pole type.
(iv) Drawback: Not self-starting; auxiliary means must be used for starting.

Methods of Self Starting:
(i) Motor Starting with an External Prime Mover:
    • Prime mover rotates rotor poles so they interchange positions with stator poles in clockwise direction.
    • Continuous unidirectional torque pulls rotor into synchronous speed; prime mover is then decoupled.
(ii) Motor Starting with Damper Windings:
    • Damper winding consists of copper bars embedded in salient pole faces, short-circuited at ends.
    • (i) 3-phase supply given to stator with rotor unenergized; motor starts as an induction motor.
    • (ii) Near synchronous speed, rotor is excited with DC; rotor poles lock in with rotating stator poles.
    • (iii) At synchronous speed, damper bars cut no flux and carry no current.

Effect of Changing Field Excitation at Constant Load:
• Normal Excitation (E_b = V): Motor operates at Unity Power Factor (cos ϕ = 1); armature current Iₐ is minimum.
• Under-Excitation (E_b < V): Motor current Iₐ lags behind V; operates at Lagging Power Factor.
• Over-Excitation (E_b > V): Motor current Iₐ leads V; operates at Leading Power Factor.

Synchronous Condenser:
• An over-excited synchronous motor running on no-load is known as a synchronous condenser.
• Takes leading current and behaves as a capacitor.
• Connected in parallel with induction motors to supply leading kVAR, neutralizing lagging reactive kVAR and raising system power factor from cos ϕ_L to cos ϕ. Widely used at major bulk supply substations.
