PART I: THREE-PHASE INDUCTION MOTOR
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

1. INTRODUCTION & WORKING PRINCIPLE
A three-phase induction motor is an asynchronous AC motor that operates on the principle of electromagnetic induction between the stationary stator and the rotating rotor. It is often referred to as a "rotating transformer" because:
- The stator acts like the primary winding of a transformer connected to an AC source.
- The rotor acts like a short-circuited secondary winding.
- Energy is transferred entirely through magnetic coupling across the air gap without any physical conductive contact.

Basic Working Principle:
1. When balanced 3-phase AC voltages are supplied to the 3-phase stator winding, an alternating current flows, creating a Rotating Magnetic Field (RMF) of constant magnitude (1.5 times the peak flux per phase) revolving at synchronous speed:
   Ns = 120 * f / P  (where f = supply frequency in Hz, P = number of stator poles).
2. The revolving stator flux cuts the stationary rotor conductors across the air gap.
3. According to Faraday's law of electromagnetic induction, an electromotive force (EMF) is induced in the rotor conductors.
4. Because the rotor circuit forms a closed electrical loop (either through end rings or external resistors), this induced EMF drives circulating rotor currents.
5. In accordance with Lorentz's force law, current-carrying rotor conductors situated in the magnetic field experience mechanical forces: F = B * I * L.
6. According to Lenz's law, the induced current and resulting electromagnetic torque act in a direction to oppose the cause producing them—which is the relative velocity between the revolving stator field and the rotor conductors.
7. Consequently, the rotor accelerates in the same direction as the rotating magnetic field, attempting to catch up with synchronous speed Ns, but always running at a slightly lower speed N.


2. CONSTRUCTIONAL DETAILS
An induction motor consists of two main assemblies:

A. Stator:
- Outer stationary frame fabricated from cast iron or welded steel plates.
- Houses a hollow cylindrical stator core built from thin, high-grade silicon steel stampings (laminations) insulated with varnish to suppress eddy current losses.
- Slots are punched along the inner periphery of the stator core to hold a distributed 3-phase star or delta connected winding.

B. Rotor:
Two primary types of rotors are used in three-phase induction motors:
1. Squirrel Cage Rotor:
   - Built from a laminated cylindrical core slotted on its outer periphery.
   - Heavy copper or aluminum conductor bars are embedded into the slots.
   - The ends of all conductor bars are permanently short-circuited on both sides by heavy continuous end-rings of the same material, forming a structure resembling a squirrel cage.
   - Slots are slightly skewed (not parallel to the shaft) to prevent magnetic locking (cogging), reduce acoustic hum, and produce a smoother starting torque.
   - Characteristics: Extremely rugged, simple, low cost, brushless, low maintenance, but possesses a fixed rotor resistance and low starting torque.
2. Slip Ring Rotor (Wound Rotor):
   - The laminated core has semi-closed slots carrying a distributed, insulated 3-phase star-connected winding wound for the exact same number of poles as the stator.
   - The open terminal ends of the three phases are brought out and connected to three insulated brass/copper slip rings mounted on the rotor shaft.
   - Carbon brushes rest on these slip rings and connect to an external star-connected 3-phase rheostat.
   - Characteristics: By inserting external resistance during starting, starting torque is maximized while starting current is limited. Once up to rated speed, the slip rings are short-circuited, allowing normal running. Higher initial cost and maintenance.

4. MATHEMATICAL FORMULAS & ROTOR QUANTITIES
• Synchronous Speed: Ns = 120 * f / P  (rpm)
• Fractional Slip (s): s = (Ns - N) / Ns
• Percentage Slip (%s): %s = [(Ns - N) / Ns] * 100
• Rotor Speed (N): N = Ns * (1 - s)
• At Standstill: N = 0, therefore slip s = 1.
• At Synchronous Speed: N = Ns, therefore slip s = 0 (Torque = 0).
• Rotor Current Frequency (f'):
  The relative speed between rotating flux and rotor is (Ns - N) = s * Ns.
  Rotor frequency f' = (s * Ns * P) / 120 = s * f.
• Rotor Induced EMF per Phase:
  - At standstill: E2 = E1 * (N2 / N1)
  - At running slip s: E2' = s * E2
• Rotor Reactance per Phase:
  - At standstill: X2 = 2 * π * f * L2
  - At running slip s: X2' = 2 * π * f' * L2 = s * (2 * π * f * L2) = s * X2
• Rotor Impedance per Phase: Z2' = sqrt(R2^2 + (s * X2)^2)
• Rotor Current per Phase: I2 = (s * E2) / sqrt(R2^2 + (s * X2)^2)
• Rotor Power Factor: cos(ϕ2) = R2 / sqrt(R2^2 + (s * X2)^2)


5. TORQUE EQUATION & CHARACTERISTICS
• General Torque Proportionality:
  T ∝ Φ * I2 * cos(ϕ2)
  Since stator flux Φ ∝ E2:
  T = K * (s * E2^2 * R2) / [R2^2 + (s * X2)^2]
• Starting Torque (Tst) [at s = 1]:
  Tst = K * (E2^2 * R2) / [R2^2 + X2^2]
• Condition for Maximum Running Torque (Breakdown / Pull-Out Torque):
  Differentiating the torque expression with respect to slip s and equating to zero:
  R2 = s * X2  =>  sm = R2 / X2
  Maximum Torque value: Tmax = K * E2^2 / (2 * X2)
  * Note: The magnitude of maximum torque is independent of rotor resistance R2; however, the slip sm at which maximum torque occurs is directly proportional to R2.
• Torque-Slip Characteristics:
  - Stable Operating Zone (Low Slip, s ≈ 0 to 0.05): (s * X2)^2 is negligible compared to R2^2.
    T ∝ s / R2  =>  Torque is directly proportional to slip (linear straight line).
  - Unstable Zone (High Slip, s approaching 1): R2^2 is negligible compared to (s * X2)^2.
    T ∝ s * R2 / (s^2 * X2^2) ∝ 1 / s  =>  Torque is inversely proportional to slip (rectangular hyperbola).


6. POWER FLOW DIAGRAM OF AN INDUCTION MOTOR
• Stator Electrical Power Input (P_in) = sqrt(3) * VL * IL * cos(ϕ)
• Stator Copper Losses (3 * I1^2 * R1) + Stator Core / Iron Losses
• Rotor Power Input (Air Gap Power, P_g or P_ir) = P_in - Stator Losses = 2 * π * Ns * Td / 60
• Rotor Copper Losses (P_cu) = 3 * I2^2 * R2 = s * P_ir
• Gross Mechanical Power Developed (P_d) = P_ir - P_cu = (1 - s) * P_ir = 2 * π * N * Td / 60
• Golden Ratio of Induction Motor Powers:
  P_ir : P_cu : P_d = 1 : s : (1 - s)
• Friction, Windage, and Mechanical Losses (P_fw)
• Net Useful Shaft Output Power (P_out) = P_d - P_fw = 2 * π * N * T_sh / 60


7. STARTING METHODS OF THREE-PHASE INDUCTION MOTORS
At standstill (s = 1), an induction motor behaves like a short-circuited transformer and draws 5 to 8 times rated full-load current at a poor lagging power factor.
1. Direct-On-Line (DOL) Starting:
   - Motor connected directly across 3-phase line voltage via contactor.
   - Suitable only for small motors (up to 5 kW to 7.5 kW) to prevent severe supply voltage dips.
2. Stator Resistance Starting:
   - External resistors inserted in series with stator lines drop terminal voltage to x * V during starting.
   - Starting current reduced to x * Isc, but starting torque drops to x^2 * Tst. Significant power wasted in resistors.
3. Autotransformer Starting:
   - Tapped 3-phase autotransformer supplies reduced voltage (x * V, typically 50%, 65%, or 80% taps) during starting.
   - Switched to full line voltage when motor reaches ~80% speed.
   - Starting torque = x^2 * Tst; line current = x^2 * Isc.
4. Star-Delta (Y-Δ) Starting:
   - Motor stator winding designed for normal running in Delta (Δ).
   - Connected in Star (Y) during start: Phase voltage is reduced to V_L / sqrt(3).
   - Starting current and starting torque are both reduced to exactly 1/3 (33.3%) of their direct-on-line delta values.
5. Rotor Resistance Starting (Slip Ring Motors Only):
   - External 3-phase star-connected variable rheostat added in series with rotor windings through slip rings.
   - Maximizes starting torque (can achieve Tmax at start if R2_total = X2) while simultaneously limiting starting current. Resistances are cut out gradually as motor speeds up.


8. SPEED CONTROL METHODS
Since rotor speed N = (120 * f / P) * (1 - s):
1. Frequency Control (V/f Control): Varying supply frequency while keeping V/f ratio constant to prevent magnetic saturation.
2. Pole Changing: Changing stator winding connections to alter pole number P (gives stepped speeds: e.g., 2, 4, 8 poles).
3. Slip Control: In slip ring motors, varying external rotor resistance changes slip and speed under load.

PART II: SYNCHRONOUS MACHINES (ALTERNATORS & SYNCHRONOUS MOTORS)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

1. INTRODUCTION & ADVANTAGES OF STATIONARY ARMATURE
A synchronous machine rotates at an unvarying synchronous speed Ns = 120 * f / P regardless of load.
Unlike DC machines, commercial alternators are built with a Stationary Armature (Stator) and a Rotating Field (Rotor):

Four Decisive Engineering Advantages of Stationary Armature:
1. High-Voltage Insulation: Alternators generate high transmission voltages (11 kV to 33 kV). It is much easier to insulate stationary slots against high voltage because windings are not subjected to centrifugal forces and ample slot space is available.
2. Direct Load Connection: The heavy 3-phase load currents (thousands of amperes) are drawn directly from fixed stator terminals without needing large, high-maintenance slip rings and carbon brushes.
3. Low-Voltage DC Field Excitation: Only two light, low-voltage (110 V to 250 V DC) slip rings are required to feed small field exciting current to the rotating poles.
4. High Mechanical Robustness & Speed: A rotating field structure has superior mechanical balance and structural rigidity, permitting high rotational speeds (1500 to 3000 rpm in turbo-alternators).

3. ARMATURE WINDING TERMS & EMF EQUATION OF ALTERNATOR
• Definitions:
  - Turn (T): Two conductors joined by an end connector.
  - Conductor (Z): The active length of wire lying in the magnetic field slots: Z = 2 * T.
  - Electrical vs. Mechanical Angles: θ_electrical = (P / 2) * θ_mechanical.
  - Pole Pitch: Angular distance between centers of adjacent poles = 180° electrical = Total Slots / P.
  - Coil Pitch (Coil Span): Distance between two coil sides of a single coil.

• Winding Factors:
  1. Pitch Factor / Chording Factor (Kp):
     - In a full-pitch coil, coil span = pole pitch = 180° electrical (Kp = 1).
     - In a short-pitch (fractional-pitch) coil, coil span is short by angle α (Kp < 1).
     - Formula: Kp = cos(α / 2)
     - Advantages of Short Pitching: Saves copper in end-connections, eliminates higher harmonics (e.g., 5th and 7th harmonics), improves output voltage waveform towards a pure sinusoid.
  2. Distribution Factor / Breadth Factor (Kd):
     - Coils belonging to one phase are distributed across several slots per pole per phase (m = Slots / (P * phases)).
     - Slot angular pitch β = 180° / (Slots / P).
     - Formula: Kd = sin(m * β / 2) / [m * sin(β / 2)]
     - Value of Kd is always less than 1 (typically 0.95 to 0.96).
  3. Total Winding Factor (Kw): Kw = Kp * Kd

• Derivation of Alternator EMF Equation:
  - Flux cut per conductor in one revolution = P * Φ Webers.
  - Time for one revolution = 60 / N seconds.
  - Average EMF induced per conductor = dΦ / dt = (P * Φ) / (60 / N) = (P * Φ * N) / 60 Volts.
  - Substituting N = 120 * f / P: Average EMF per conductor = 2 * f * Φ Volts.
  - For a full-pitch, concentrated winding with Z conductors in series per phase:
    Average EMF per phase = 2 * f * Φ * Z.
  - For a sine wave, Form Factor = RMS / Average = 1.11.
    RMS EMF per phase = 1.11 * (2 * f * Φ * Z) = 2.22 * f * Φ * Z Volts.
  - Expressed in terms of Turns per phase (T = Z / 2):
    RMS EMF per phase = 4.44 * f * Φ * T Volts.
  - Accounting for pitch factor (Kp) and distribution factor (Kd):
    E_ph = 4.44 * Kp * Kd * f * Φ * T  Volts
    (Or E_ph = 2.22 * Kp * Kd * f * Φ * Z  Volts).


4. VOLTAGE REGULATION OF AN ALTERNATOR
• Definition:
  The percentage rise in terminal voltage when full rated load at a specified power factor is thrown off, while keeping excitation and prime-mover speed constant:
  % Voltage Regulation = [(E0 - V) / V] * 100
  (Where E0 is no-load phase voltage and V is rated full-load terminal phase voltage).

• Causes of Voltage Drop in Loaded Alternator:
  1. Armature Resistance Drop (Ia * Ra): True AC effective resistance is ~1.5 times DC resistance due to skin effect.
  2. Armature Leakage Reactance Drop (Ia * XL): Caused by leakage flux linking armature conductors.
  3. Armature Reaction: Effect of armature flux on main field flux:
     - At Unity Power Factor (Resistive load): Cross-magnetizing (distorts field).
     - At Lagging Power Factor (Inductive load): Demagnetizing (drastically reduces terminal voltage; positive regulation).
     - At Leading Power Factor (Capacitive load): Magnetizing (increases terminal voltage; negative regulation).

• Synchronous Impedance (EMF) Method:
  - From Open-Circuit Test (OCC) and Short-Circuit Test (SCC) at the same field current If:
    Synchronous Impedance: Zs = Voc / Isc
    Synchronous Reactance: Xs = sqrt(Zs^2 - Ra^2)
  - Generated No-Load EMF per phase:
    E0 = sqrt([V * cos(ϕ) + Ia * Ra]^2 + [V * sin(ϕ) ± Ia * Xs]^2)
    * Use (+) sign for Lagging Power Factor.
    * Use (-) sign for Leading Power Factor.
    * Use ϕ = 0 (cos ϕ = 1, sin ϕ = 0) for Unity Power Factor.


5. SYNCHRONOUS MOTOR & SYNCHRONOUS CONDENSER
• Characteristics:
  - Runs strictly at synchronous speed Ns = 120 * f / P from no-load to full-load.
  - Not self-starting: Because of high rotor inertia, the rapidly reversing stator RMF (50 Hz = 100 reversals/sec) produces alternating torque pulses with zero average starting torque.
• Methods of Starting:
  1. Auxiliary Motor (Pony Motor): Small induction motor spins the rotor to near synchronous speed before DC field is switched on.
  2. Damper Windings (Amortisseur Windings): Heavy copper bars embedded in the pole faces of salient poles, short-circuited at ends. Stator RMF induces currents in damper bars, starting the machine as an induction motor. Near Ns, DC excitation is applied, pulling the rotor into magnetic synchronism.
• Behavior under Load:
  When mechanical load is applied, the rotor poles fall back behind the stator field poles by a torque / power angle α (or δ). This creates a resultant voltage Er = V - Eb, causing armature current Ia = Er / Zs to rise and supply load torque.
• Effect of Field Excitation at Constant Load:
  - Normal Excitation (Eb ≈ V): Armature current Ia is minimum; operates at Unity Power Factor (cos ϕ = 1).
  - Under-Excitation (Eb < V): Armature current Ia increases and lags voltage V; operates at Lagging Power Factor.
  - Over-Excitation (Eb > V): Armature current Ia increases and leads voltage V; operates at Leading Power Factor (supplies reactive power).
• Synchronous Condenser:
  - An over-excited synchronous motor running without any mechanical load on its shaft.
  - Takes a leading current, behaving like a large static capacitor bank.
  - Connected across power lines at main grid substations to supply leading kVAR, neutralizing lagging reactive power drawn by industrial induction motors and improving the overall grid power factor.
