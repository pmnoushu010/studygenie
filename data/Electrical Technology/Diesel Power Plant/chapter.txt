ELECTRICAL TECHNOLOGY - MODULE 3: DIESEL POWER PLANT

1. INTRODUCTION & WORKING PRINCIPLE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
A Diesel Power Plant (also known as a Diesel Electric Power Plant or DG Set Power Station) is an internal combustion (IC) engine-based electrical generating facility. It utilizes a heavy-duty multi-cylinder compression-ignition (diesel) engine as the prime mover coupled directly to an AC synchronous generator (alternator) to produce electrical power.

Basic Operating Mechanism:
• Diesel fuel is injected into the engine cylinders under high pressure at the end of the compression stroke, where it auto-ignites in the presence of highly compressed, hot air.
• The combustion of diesel fuel produces an intense release of thermal energy, creating extreme temperatures (1500°C to 2000°C) and high gas pressures inside the cylinder.
• These high-pressure combustion gases expand against the piston crown, driving the reciprocating motion of the piston.
• The reciprocating motion of the piston is converted into continuous rotary mechanical power at the engine crankshaft via the connecting rod and crank mechanism.
• The rotating crankshaft drives the rotor of the direct-coupled synchronous generator (alternator), producing 3-phase AC electrical energy at standardized voltage and frequency.


2. ESSENTIAL SYSTEMS OF A DIESEL POWER PLANT
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
A modern industrial diesel power plant comprises seven interconnected subsystems:

1. Diesel Engine (Prime Mover):
   - Heavy-duty, 2-stroke or 4-stroke multi-cylinder compression ignition engine.
   - May be naturally aspirated, supercharged, or turbocharged to increase power density and thermal efficiency.

2. Fuel Supply and Handling System:
   - Stores, pumps, filters, and delivers clean fuel oil to the engine cylinders at the required injection pressure.
   - Key Components:
     * Main Fuel Storage Tank: Large outdoor bulk storage tank receiving fuel supplies from road or rail tankers.
     * Fuel Transfer Pump: Transfers fuel oil from the main storage tank to the daily service tank.
     * Strainers and Primary Fuel Filter: Remove coarse dirt, sand, and particulate matter to protect transfer pumps and fuel lines.
     * All-Day Tank (Day Service Tank): An elevated indoor tank holding sufficient fuel to run the plant for 24 hours of continuous operation. An overflow return line safely recirculates excess fuel back to the bulk storage tank.
     * Fine Secondary Fuel Filter: Traps micro-particles before fuel enters the precision fuel injection equipment.
     * High-Pressure Fuel Injection Pump (FIP): Pressurizes fuel oil (150 to 300 bar or higher in common-rail systems) and meters exact fuel quantity per cylinder.
     * Fuel Injectors / Atomizers: Spray finely atomized fuel into the combustion chamber through microscopic multi-hole nozzles.

3. Air Intake System:
   - Delivers fresh, clean, and cool ambient air required for stoichiometric fuel combustion inside the engine cylinders.
   - Key Components:
     * Air Intake Duct / Louvers: Draws air from outside the powerhouse building.
     * Air Filter (Dry or Oil-Bath type): Traps atmospheric dust, soot, and abrasive airborne particles to prevent cylinder liner and piston ring wear.
     * Turbocharger / Supercharger (Optional): Compresses incoming air using exhaust gas energy to increase air density, boost engine volumetric efficiency, and increase power output.

4. Engine Starting System:
   - Unlike small automotive engines that use battery-driven starter motors, large industrial and utility-scale diesel engines require high cranking torque to overcome huge inertia and high compression ratios.
   - Compressed Air Starting System:
     * Motor-driven air compressor pressurizes air to 20 to 30 bar.
     * High-pressure air receiver (air storage tank) stores starting air.
     * Pneumatic distributor valve admits high-pressure air sequentially into the engine cylinders during the power stroke, spinning the crankshaft until the engine fires on fuel.

5. Exhaust System:
   - Safely collects, silences, and discharges hot combustion flue gases into the atmosphere away from personnel and ventilation air intakes.
   - Key Components:
     * Exhaust Manifold: Collects gases from each cylinder head.
     * Exhaust Piping with Thermal Expansion Bellows: Accommodates thermal expansion and reduces engine vibration transfer to the structure.
     * Silencer (Muffler): Significantly reduces high-decibel acoustic noise caused by high-velocity pulsating exhaust gases.
     * Waste Heat Recovery Boiler / Economizer (Optional): Captures waste heat from 400°C–500°C exhaust gases to generate low-pressure process steam or hot water, raising overall plant thermal efficiency.

6. Cooling System:
   - Combustion temperatures inside the cylinder peak between 1500°C and 2000°C. About 30% of total fuel heat energy is absorbed by cylinder walls and cylinder heads.
   - Without effective continuous cooling, cylinder liners warp, piston crowns overheat, lubricating oil oxidizes and burns, and metal seizure occurs.
   - Dual-Circuit Cooling Circuit:
     * Engine Water Jacket: Treated, soft demineralized water circulates through cylinder block jackets and cylinder heads to absorb rejected engine heat.
     * Surge Tank: Accommodates thermal expansion of cooling water, releases air bubbles, and ensures a constant positive head for the water pump.
     * Shell-and-Tube Heat Exchanger: The hot closed-loop engine jacket water transfers its heat to an open secondary raw water loop.
     * Raw Water Circuit & Cooling Tower: Raw water pump circulates cold water through the heat exchanger to absorb heat and sprays it into an induced-draft cooling tower to cool it for reuse.

7. Lubricating System:
   - Minimizes mechanical friction, reduces component wear, cools moving parts, cleans away wear debris, and seals combustion gaps.
   - Parts Lubricated: Piston rings, cylinder liners, crankshaft main bearings, crankpin bearings, gudgeon pins, camshaft bearings, timing gears, and valve rockers.
   - Key Components:
     * Lubricating Oil Sump / Tank: Stores engine oil.
     * Engine-Driven Oil Pump: Draws oil from the sump and circulates it through the engine galleries at regulated pressure.
     * Oil Strainer & Bypass Filter: Continuously filters out metallic particles, carbon soot, and sludge.
     * Lubricating Oil Cooler: Shell-and-tube cooler using cooling water to extract heat from lubricating oil, maintaining oil viscosity and preventing thermal degradation.


3. ADVANTAGES AND DISADVANTAGES OF DIESEL POWER PLANTS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Advantages:
1. Compact Space Requirement & Rapid Installation:
   - Occupies significantly less land area and requires lighter civil foundations than equivalent steam or hydro power plants.
2. Quick Start and Load Pick-up:
   - Can be started from cold condition, synchronized, and put on full load within 2 to 5 minutes, making it unmatched for emergency and peak-load duties.
3. High Thermal Efficiency in Small/Medium Capacities:
   - Overall thermal efficiency ranges from 35% to 42%, which is higher than comparable steam turbine plants in the small-to-medium capacity bracket (up to 150 MW).
4. Excellent Part-Load Efficiency & Load Following:
   - Multiple smaller generating units can be switched on/off to match fluctuating daily load profiles with high efficiency.
5. Limited Cooling Water Requirements:
   - Requires far less water than a steam power plant of identical capacity because it does not require condenser cooling.
6. Simple Plant Layout & Fuel Storage:
   - Compact liquid fuel storage without large coal storage yards, boiler houses, or tall chimneys.
7. Clean Operation & Zero Ash Handling:
   - Operates cleanly on liquid fuel; no coal pulverizers, ash ponds, electrostatic precipitators, or ash disposal problems.
8. Reduced Supervision & Staffing:
   - Fully automated start/stop, governing, and protection systems require fewer operators.

Disadvantages:
1. High Operating & Fuel Costs:
   - Diesel fuel is considerably more expensive per unit of heat energy than coal or natural gas.
2. High Lubrication and Maintenance Expenses:
   - Continuous mechanical wear on reciprocating components necessitates frequent oil changes, ring replacements, and routine overhauls.
3. Higher Capital Cost per kW at Large Capacities:
   - While economical at small ratings, the capital cost per installed kilowatt becomes very high for ratings beyond 50 MW.
4. Relatively Shorter Plant Operating Life:
   - Severe mechanical stresses, reciprocating vibration, and thermal cycling limit plant service life to 10–15 years (compared to 30–40 years for steam or hydro plants).
5. Serious Acoustic and Vibration Pollution:
   - Loud exhaust pulsations and mechanical clatter require heavy acoustic enclosures and vibration-damping pads.
6. Limited Unit Size and Capacity:
   - Individual diesel engine units rarely exceed 20 MW to 30 MW; diesel plants cannot be practically built for massive central baseload grid generation (e.g., 500 MW to 1000 MW).


4. TYPICAL APPLICATIONS IN MODERN POWER SYSTEMS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
• Standby / Emergency Power Plants:
  Installed in critical institutions such as hospitals, data centers, airports, telecommunication exchanges, continuous-process chemical plants, and high-rise commercial complexes to provide uninterrupted power during grid outages.
• Peak Load Plants:
  Operated during brief daily peak demand intervals when grid demand exceeds baseload thermal and hydro capacity.
• Base Load Plants for Remote / Island Locations:
  Ideal for isolated islands, hilly terrains, mines, and remote rural microgrids where grid transmission line construction is economically unfeasible.
• Mobile Portable Power Units:
  Mounted on trailers or barges for disaster relief, temporary construction sites, and military deployments.
• Nursery Stations:
  Installed in emerging townships to establish a local distribution network before a high-voltage national grid transmission line arrives.
