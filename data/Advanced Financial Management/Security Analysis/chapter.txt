1. INTRODUCTION TO SECURITY ANALYSIS
================================================================================
Security Analysis involves a systematic evaluation of the risk-return profiles of various financial assets to help rational investors estimate the intrinsic value of a company from all available price-sensitive information. The ultimate objective is to identify market mispricings—buying underpriced securities and selling or shorting overpriced securities to earn a superior risk-adjusted return.

Two broad analytical disciplines are employed in security analysis:
1. Fundamental Analysis: Examines economic, industry, and firm-specific fundamentals to determine the true "intrinsic value" of a security based on discounted future cash flows and dividend streams.
2. Technical Analysis: Studies historical market trading data (prices, volumes, moving averages, and chart patterns) on the premise that market psychology and price trends repeat over time.

2. FUNDAMENTAL ANALYSIS & VALUATION MODELS
================================================================================
2.1 The Intrinsic Value Concept:
Fundamental analysis operates on the core axiom that the share price depends upon the present value of future dividends expected by the shareholders discounted at an appropriate risk-adjusted capitalization rate ($k_e$). This discounted present value is defined as the "Intrinsic Value" of the share.
• If Current Market Price ($P_0$) < Intrinsic Value ($V_0$): The security is UNDERPRICED (Undervalued) -> Recommendation: BUY.
• If Current Market Price ($P_0$) > Intrinsic Value ($V_0$): The security is OVERPRICED (Overvalued) -> Recommendation: SELL.
• If Current Market Price ($P_0$) = Intrinsic Value ($V_0$): The security is FAIRLY PRICED -> Recommendation: HOLD.

2.2 Dividend Valuation Formulations:
1. Uniform Perpetual Dividend:
   If a firm pays a constant dividend of ₹ D per share perpetually:
   $$P_0 = \frac{D}{k_e}$$

2. Constant Dividend Growth Model (Gordon's Model):
   Where dividends grow at a constant annual compounded rate $g$, such that $D_1 = D_0(1+g)$:
   $$P_0 = \frac{D_1}{k_e - g} = \frac{D_0(1 + g)}{k_e - g} \quad (\text{Condition: } k_e > g)$$
   Where:
   - $P_0$ = Intrinsic price of the equity share today
   - $D_0$ = Dividend per share (DPS) paid in the current period
   - $D_1$ = Expected dividend per share at the end of Period 1
   - $k_e$ = Desired / Required rate of return on the equity share
   - $g$ = Expected perpetual annual growth rate in dividends ($g = b \times r$)

3. Fundamental P/E Multiple Formulation:
   Relating price to earnings via the dividend payout ratio ($b = D_1 / E_1$):
   $$P_0 = \frac{b \times E_1}{k_e - g} = \frac{b \times E_0(1 + g)}{k_e - g}$$
   Dividing both sides by next year's earnings per share ($E_1$), the Fundamental P/E Multiple is:
   $$\frac{P_0}{E_1} = \frac{b}{k_e - g}$$

3. THE E-I-C FRAMEWORK (FUNDAMENTAL ANALYSIS STAGES)
================================================================================
Fundamental analysis encompasses three concentric circles representing the sequence of an investment decision:
   [ Economic Analysis ]  -->  [ Industry Analysis ]  -->  [ Company Analysis ]

3.1 Economic Analysis:
Monitors macroeconomic health and aggregate growth prospects that dictate industrial demand and capital market liquidity.
• Key Macro Factors:
  (a) GDP Growth Rates: Differentiating between nominal GDP and real GDP (adjusted for inflation).
  (b) Industrial Sector Growth: Measured via Index of Industrial Production (IIP).
  (c) Inflation Rates: Wholesale Price Index (WPI) and Consumer Price Index (CPI). Inflation shifts purchasing power, increases borrowing costs, and distorts historical depreciation.
  (d) Monsoon & Agriculture: Agricultural output provides forward linkages (raw materials for agro-industries) and backward linkages (rural consumer demand for FMCG, automobiles, and equipment).
  (e) Money Supply & Liquidity: M3 growth influences bank credit availability, interest rates, and inflation. Equity investments act as an inflation hedge over long horizons.

• Forecasting Techniques in Economic Analysis:
  1. Anticipatory Surveys: Aggregates expert forecasts and business intentions regarding capital expenditures, inventory buildups, and consumer spending. Limitations: Surveyed intentions may not materialize and lack statistical forecast precision.
  2. Barometer / Indicator Approach:
     - Leading Indicators: Variables that reach turning points (peaks or troughs) prior to the aggregate economy (e.g., stock market indices, building permits, new business orders).
     - Coincidental Indicators: Variables that move in tandem with aggregate economic performance (e.g., GDP, retail sales, industrial production).
     - Lagging Indicators: Variables that change direction only after the aggregate economy has altered course (e.g., unemployment duration, commercial lending rates, inventory-to-sales ratios).
     - Diffusion / Composite Index: Combines multiple indicators into a consolidated index to eliminate localized anomalies.
  3. Economic Model Building: Uses econometric and national accounting frameworks (GNP modeling) to estimate aggregate demand components:
     $$Y = C + I + G + (X - M)$$

3.2 Industry Analysis:
Examines structural characteristics, cost structures, and competitive forces governing specific industry sectors.
• Key Industry Factors:
  (a) Product Life Cycle (PLC):
      - Pioneering / Initial Stage: Rapid sales growth, high risk, high initial margins.
      - Expansion / Growth Stage: Solid volume growth, established competitors, high profitability.
      - Maturity Stage: Saturation, intense price competition, moderate and steady margins.
      - Decline Stage: Technological obsolescence or substitute products, falling profits.
  (b) Demand-Supply Gap: Capacity additions vs. demand growth dictate unit price realization.
  (c) Barriers to Entry: Capital intensity, proprietary patents, distribution networks, economies of scale.
  (d) Government Attitude & Regulation: Subsidies, price controls, tariffs, PLI schemes.
  (e) State of Competition: Homogeneous vs. differentiated products; growth vs. cyclical vs. defensive industries.
  (f) Cost Structure & Profitability: Analysis of Gross Profit Ratio, Operating Margin, Return on Equity (ROE), and Return on Capital Employed (ROCE).
  (g) Technology & R&D: Susceptibility to rapid technological disruption and obsolescence.

• Techniques in Industry Analysis:
  1. Regression Analysis: Relates industry sales/demand (dependent variable) to macroeconomic drivers like GDP, disposable income, and demographic shifts (independent variables).
  2. Input-Output Analysis: Studies cross-sector flow of commodities to detect structural growth or decline across backward/forward linked industries.

3.3 Company Analysis:
Assesses the quantitative financial performance and qualitative competitive moats of an individual firm.
• Quantitative Financial Evaluation:
  (a) Net Worth & Book Value: Total Net Worth = Equity Capital + Preference Capital + Free Reserves - Intangibles - Fictitious Assets - Accumulated Losses. Book Value per Share = Net Worth / Outstanding Equity Shares. Limitation: Historical asset values do not reflect replacement cost or future earning capacity.
  (b) Sources & Uses of Funds (Funds Flow Analysis): Evaluates whether short-term funds have been recklessly deployed to finance long-term illiquid assets (maturity mismatch leading to liquidity crisis).
  (c) Financial Statement Analysis: Cross-sectional benchmarking against industry peers and longitudinal time-series trend analysis (operating efficiency, leverage, liquidity ratios).
  (d) Growth Record & Capital Expenditure: Evaluates growth in Net Block (plant and machinery capacity expansion), EPS growth, and P/E relative to peer averages.

• Qualitative Fundamentals:
  (a) Quality of Management: Integrity, capital allocation discipline, transparency in investor conference calls (CEO/CFO Q&A sessions), and Management Discussion & Analysis (MD&A) reports.
  (b) Corporate Governance: Full compliance with SEBI (Listing Obligations and Disclosure Requirements) Regulations 2015, induction of truly independent directors, and institutional holding patterns.
  (c) Competitive Advantage (Economic Moat): Sustained cost leadership, brand equity, high switching costs, and network effects (e.g., Apple, Samsung).
  (d) Marketability & Liquidity: Public float dispersal, trading volumes, and bid-ask spreads.
  (e) Group Analysis: Group company exposure norms and promoter share pledging disclosures mandated by SEBI and RBI.

4. TECHNICAL ANALYSIS: PRINCIPLES, THEORIES & PATTERNS
================================================================================
4.1 Definition & Core Premise:
Technical Analysis is the study of historical market action—primarily through price charts, volume trends, and mathematical indicators—to forecast future price direction. It focuses on investor psychology and demand-supply equilibrium rather than accounting statements.

4.2 Four Foundational Assumptions:
1. The market value of a security is determined solely by the interaction of demand and supply.
2. Supply and demand are governed by numerous rational and irrational market factors.
3. Security prices move in persistent trends that endure over measurable periods of time.
4. Changes in trends are caused by shifts in demand-supply balance, which can be detected through chart patterns.

4.3 Three Guiding Principles:
1. The Market Discounts Everything: All fundamental, macroeconomic, political, and psychological information is instantaneously absorbed into the market price.
2. Price Moves in Trends: Prices do not move randomly from day to day; once established, a trend is more likely to continue than reverse.
3. History Tends to Repeat Itself: Because human psychology and crowd emotions (fear and greed) are invariant over time, chart patterns repeat reliably.

4.4 Theories of Technical Analysis:
1. The Dow Theory (Charles Dow):
   • Two averages must confirm: The Dow Jones Industrial Average (DJIA) and Dow Jones Transportation Average (DJTA) must validate trend breakouts simultaneously.
   • Three Market Movements:
     - Primary Trend: Long-term major trend lasting from 1 year to 36 months or more (Bull or Bear market).
     - Secondary Reaction: Intermediate corrections against the primary trend lasting 2 weeks to a few months (retracing 33% to 66% of the primary move).
     - Daily Fluctuations: Day-to-day noise lasting hours to days (not reliable for forecasting).
   • Three Phases of a Primary Bull Market:
     - Phase 1 (Accumulation): Astute, knowledgeable investors quietly accumulate shares at depressed prices amidst widespread economic pessimism.
     - Phase 2 (Public Participation / Earnings Expansion): Corporate earnings improve, economic outlook stabilizes, and institutional/retail participants enter the market.
     - Phase 3 (Rampant Speculation / Distribution): Widespread euphoria, speculative buying, and heavy media coverage. Astute investors begin offloading (distributing) their holdings to the public.

2. Elliot Wave Theory (Ralph Nelson Elliot, 1934):
   • Based on 75 years of stock price patterns and nature's Fibonacci sequences.
   • Complete Market Cycle consists of 8 Waves:
     - 5 Impulsive Waves (Numbered 1, 2, 3, 4, 5): Move in the direction of the main trend.
       * Waves 1, 3, 5 are directional upward impulses.
       * Waves 2 and 4 are corrective pullbacks within the basic uptrend.
     - 3 Corrective Waves (Lettered a, b, c): Counter-trend correction against the main advance.
       * Wave a moves against the primary trend.
       * Wave b is a counter-trend retracement rally.
       * Wave c is the final downward corrective wave.

3. Random Walk Theory:
   • Developed by statisticians including M.G. Kendall and popularized by Burton Malkiel.
   • Asserts that successive stock price changes are serially independent and behave like a roulette wheel or a drunkard's walk in a blind lane.
   • Successive peaks and troughs are statistical accidents; past price patterns hold zero predictive validity for future returns.

4.5 Charting Formations & Price Patterns:
• Chart Types:
  - Line Chart: Simple plot connecting successive daily closing prices.
  - Bar Chart (OHLC): Vertical line represents High to Low; left notch represents Opening price; right notch represents Closing price.
  - Japanese Candlestick: Rectangular real body depicts Open-Close; upper and lower shadows (wicks) depict High-Low extremes. White/Green body = Bullish ($Close > Open$); Black/Red body = Bearish ($Close < Open$); Doji = Open equals Close (cross/plus sign indicating market indecision).
  - Point & Figure (P&F) Chart: Eliminates the time axis. Uses columns of 'X' for price rises and 'O' for price declines based on a predefined Box Size (e.g., ₹1) and Reversal Criterion (e.g., 3 boxes).

• Key Chart Formations:
  - Support & Resistance: Support represents a price floor where buying interest halts declines; Resistance represents a price ceiling where selling pressure arrests advances. A decisive breakout beyond these levels establishes a new trend.
  - Channels & Wedges: Channels feature parallel trendlines; wedges feature converging trendlines.
  - Head and Shoulders (Top Pattern): Reliable major bearish reversal pattern consisting of a Left Shoulder, a higher Head, and a lower Right Shoulder. A decisive penetration below the Neckline confirms trend reversal. Target Price Drop = Vertical distance from peak of Head to Neckline subtracted from breakout point.
  - Inverse Head and Shoulders: Major bullish reversal pattern forming at the base of a downtrend; breakout above Neckline signals aggressive upward rally.
  - Triangles / Coils: Symmetrical, ascending, or descending triangles indicating consolidating volatility prior to a sharp breakout.
  - Flags and Pennants: Short-term continuation patterns resembling flags on a mast, representing a brief consolidation before the prior trend resumes.
  - Double Top ("M" Pattern): Bearish reversal signaling exhaustion at identical price peaks.
  - Double Bottom ("W" Pattern): Bullish reversal signaling price support at identical price troughs.
  - Price Gaps: Unfilled spaces between consecutive trading periods (Breakaway, Runaway/Measuring, and Exhaustion gaps).

4.6 Technical Market Indicators:
1. Breadth Index:
   $$\text{Breadth Index} = \frac{\text{Net Advances or Declines}}{\text{Total Number of Issues Traded}}$$
   Measures market breadth across all listed securities. Validates or contradicts moves in blue-chip indices (DJIA/Nifty).
2. Volume of Transactions:
   - Price Rise + Increasing Volume = Bullish confirmation (strong accumulation).
   - Price Decline + Increasing Volume = Bearish confirmation (heavy distribution).
   - Price Rise + Decreasing Volume = Bullish exhaustion / Bearish divergence.
   - Price Decline + Decreasing Volume = Selling exhaustion.
3. Confidence Index (Barron's Confidence Index):
   $$\text{Confidence Index} = \frac{\text{Yield on High-Grade Bonds}}{\text{Yield on Low-Grade / Speculative Bonds}}$$
   A rising ratio reflects investor confidence and appetite for risk, serving as a leading indicator of equity market rallies.
4. Relative Strength Analysis:
   Ratios comparing the performance of an individual stock against an industry index or the broad market. Strong relative performers tend to sustain outperformance over intermediate horizons.
5. Odd-Lot Theory:
   A contrary-opinion indicator assuming that small, retail odd-lot traders (transacting fewer than 100 shares) are consistently incorrect at crucial market turning points.

4.7 Moving Average Models:
1. Arithmetic Moving Average (AMA):
   Simple unweighted moving average over an $n$-period window:
   $$\text{AMA}_{n,t} = \frac{P_t + P_{t-1} + \dots + P_{t-(n-1)}}{n}$$
   - 200-day / 30-week AMA: Long-term secular trend.
   - 60-day AMA: Intermediate trend.
   - 10-day AMA: Short-term tactical trend.

2. Exponential Moving Average (EMA):
   Assigns exponentially decreasing weights to older prices, placing highest priority on recent prices:
   $$\text{EMA}_t = \text{EMA}_{t-1} + a \times (P_t - \text{EMA}_{t-1})$$
   $$\text{or } \text{EMA}_t = a P_t + (1 - a) \text{EMA}_{t-1}$$
   Where Smoothing Factor (Exponent) $a$:
   $$a = \frac{2}{n + 1}$$
   - Trading Signals:
     * Buy Signal: Price crosses above a flattening or rising moving average line; or pulls back to a rising moving average and bounces upward.
     * Sell Signal: Price penetrates below a flattening or declining moving average line; or rallies to a declining moving average and fails to break through.

5. EFFICIENT MARKET HYPOTHESIS (EMH)
================================================================================
5.1 Origin & Definition:
Formulated by Prof. Eugene Fama (1960s, University of Chicago), the Efficient Market Hypothesis asserts that financial markets are informationally efficient: at any given moment, security prices fully and instantaneously reflect all available price-sensitive information. Consequently, no investor can systematically earn abnormal risk-adjusted returns ("beat the market").

5.2 Three Forms of Market Efficiency:
1. Weak-Form Efficiency:
   • Information Set: All historical trading data (past prices, trading volumes, bid-ask spreads).
   • Implication: Past price movements contain zero information regarding future price trajectories.
   • Consequence: Technical analysis cannot generate superior abnormal returns.
   • Empirical Tests:
     (a) Serial Correlation Test: Tests whether returns at time $t$ correlate with returns at $t-k$. Empirical results reveal near-zero serial correlation across short holding periods.
     (b) Run Test: Non-parametric statistical test counting the number of unbroken directional runs ($+$, $-$, or $0$) to verify whether price sequences conform to a random distribution:
         $$\mu_r = \frac{2 n_1 n_2}{n_1 + n_2} + 1$$
         $$\sigma_r = \sqrt{\frac{2 n_1 n_2 (2 n_1 n_2 - n_1 - n_2)}{(n_1 + n_2)^2 (n_1 + n_2 - 1)}}$$
     (c) Filter Rules Test: Tests trading rules buying after an $N\%$ price advance and shorting after an $N\%$ decline. Net of brokerage fees, filter rules fail to beat a simple Buy-and-Hold strategy.

2. Semi-Strong Form Efficiency:
   • Information Set: All publicly available information (financial statements, earnings announcements, dividend changes, rights issues, management commentary, economic forecasts).
   • Implication: Stock prices adjust instantaneously and without bias to public news announcements.
   • Consequence: Neither Technical Analysis nor Fundamental Analysis can systematically yield abnormal returns.
   • Empirical Event Studies:
     - Fama, Fisher, Jensen & Roll (1969): Investigated 940 stock splits; verified that prices adjust to the information content prior to or immediately at the announcement date.
     - Ball & Brown (1968): Verified price adjustments around annual earnings releases.
   • Market Anomalies / Inefficiencies:
     - Post-Earnings Announcement Drift (PEAD): Prices exhibit delayed reaction to unexpected earnings surprises.
     - Small-Firm Effect (Banz): Small-cap stocks earn persistent positive risk-adjusted excess returns over large caps.
     - Low P/E / Value Effect (Basu): Stocks with low P/E multiples systematically outperform high P/E growth stocks.
     - Calendar Anomalies: Weekend / Monday effect (negative returns on Mondays) and January effect.

3. Strong-Form Efficiency:
   • Information Set: All information, whether publicly available or privately held (insider information).
   • Implication: Prices reflect all inside secrets and proprietary corporate data.
   • Consequence: Even corporate insiders cannot earn abnormal returns.
   • Empirical Evidence: Strong-form efficiency DOES NOT HOLD in practice. Studies of corporate insiders and stock exchange specialists demonstrate consistent abnormal gains, leading to strict insider trading regulations globally.

5.3 Key Challenges to EMH:
1. Information Inadequacy & Misinformation: Information is neither freely available nor symmetrically distributed; corporate managements often selectively disclose or spin data.
2. Limited Information Processing Capacity: Herbert Simon's Bounded Rationality notes that human cognition can process only ~1,000 bits per second against millions of data bits generated.
3. Investor Irrationality & Behavioral Bias: J.M. Keynes pointed out that markets resemble "beauty contests" driven by crowd psychology, panic, and animal spirits rather than mathematical intrinsic values.
4. Monopolistic Market Power: Institutional investors and sovereign wealth funds exert asymmetric influence over market order flow and price determination.
