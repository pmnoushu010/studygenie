1. IDENTIFICATION OF TYPES OF RISK FACED BY AN ORGANIZATION
================================================================================
Risk is inherent in every corporate enterprise. Organizations face multiple dimensions of risk that threaten their survival, earnings, and competitive positioning:

1.1 Strategic Risk:
A strategic risk arises when a company's business plan or strategy becomes obsolete, less effective, or fails to adapt to dynamic market realities.
Causes include:
• Disruptive technological shifts.
• Entry of agile, aggressive competitors.
• Fundamental shifts in consumer demand patterns.
• Supply chain and raw material cost inflation.
Real-World Examples:
• Kodak: Developed the digital camera by 1975, but viewed it as a cannibalizing threat to its lucrative film business, ultimately leading to bankruptcy when competitors led the digital revolution.
• Nokia: Delayed adopting touchscreen smartphones, enabling Samsung and Apple to seize market leadership.
• Xerox: Successfully mitigated strategic risk when laser printing emerged by quickly transforming its business model to adopt laser technology.

1.2 Compliance Risk:
Every business operates within legal frameworks (Companies Act, 2013, SEBI regulations, FEMA, environmental laws). Non-compliance results in severe monetary penalties, reputational damage, and criminal liability.
Compliance risk surges when a company enters a new geographical territory, state, or product line where statutory regulations differ significantly (e.g., a cement company entering the sugar business in a different state subject to distinct agricultural and state licensing laws).

1.3 Operational Risk:
Internal risk resulting from inadequate or failed internal processes, people, and systems:
• People Risk: Human error (e.g., an accountant accidentally transferring ₹1,00,000 instead of ₹10,000), fraud, lack of training, or rogue trading.
• Process Risk: Flaws in software systems, unsegregated duties, lack of maker-checker controls, or IT infrastructure outages.
Mitigation: Dual-authorization controls, automated transaction anomaly detection, and regular internal audits.

1.4 Financial Risk:
Unexpected volatility in financial conditions such as asset prices, exchange rates, credit ratings, and interest rates. Major categories include:

a. Counterparty Risk (Credit Risk):
   Risk of non-performance or contractual default by a counterparty (failure to deliver goods after receiving payment, or default on principal/interest repayment).
b. Political Risk (Country Risk):
   Adverse actions by host governments affecting overseas investments:
   • Expropriation or confiscation of foreign assets.
   • Restrictions on currency conversion and foreign exchange rationing.
   • Moratorium on profit repatriation to the parent country.
   • Compulsory price controls and invalidation of intellectual property/patents.
c. Interest Rate Risk:
   Volatility in assets and liabilities resulting from fluctuations in market interest rates. Particularly critical for banks and leveraged corporations managing floating vs. fixed rate debt.
d. Currency (Foreign Exchange) Risk:
   Uncertainty in operating cash flows due to unpredictable fluctuations in foreign exchange rates:
   • Exporters (e.g., Infosys): Benefit when domestic currency depreciates, lose when it appreciates.
   • Importers (e.g., Indian Oil Corporation): Face cash outflow spikes when domestic currency depreciates.
e. Liquidity Risk:
   Inability of an enterprise to meet its maturing debt liabilities when due, arising from cash flow timing mismatches or sudden market illiquidity.

2. EVALUATION OF FINANCIAL RISK
================================================================================
Financial risk must be evaluated through three distinct institutional lenses:

1. Stakeholder's Perspective:
   Equity shareholders bear residual risk. High financial gearing (debt-to-equity ratio) amplifies earnings volatility and places equity holders at the bottom of liquidation priority in winding up. Lenders also monitor gearing to evaluate probability of default.

2. Company's Perspective:
   Excessive borrowing or lending to defaulting counterparties can trigger a solvency crisis, credit rating downgrades, insolvency proceedings, and forced liquidation.

3. Government's Perspective:
   Systemic financial risk can destabilize the economy. Failures of major systemic financial institutions (e.g., Lehman Brothers) destroy public trust, trigger credit crunches, and lead to sovereign debt contagion.

3. VALUE-AT-RISK (VAR)
================================================================================
Value at Risk (VAR) is the premier quantitative statistical technique used globally to measure the downside market risk of an investment, portfolio, or trading book.

Under normal market conditions, VAR answers two fundamental questions:
1. What is the worst-case scenario loss?
2. What is the maximum dollar/rupee loss over a given time horizon at a specific confidence level?

3.1 Key Features of VAR:
1. Three Core Components of Calculation:
   • Time Period (Holding Period / Horizon): E.g., 1 day, 10 days, 1 month.
   • Confidence Level: Statistically, 95% ($Z = 1.65$) or 99% ($Z = 2.33$).
   • Loss Magnitude: Expressed in monetary value (₹) or as a percentage of total portfolio value.
2. Statistical Basis: Relies on the Standard Deviation ($\sigma$) of asset price returns under a normal distribution assumption.
3. Z-Score Multiplication:
   $$\text{Daily VAR} = \text{Portfolio Value} \times \sigma_{\text{daily}} \times Z$$
4. Time Horizon Scaling (Square Root of Time Rule):
   Under the assumption of independent, identically distributed daily returns:
   $$\text{VAR}_T = \text{VAR}_1 \times \sqrt{T}$$
   Where $T$ is the number of trading days (e.g., $\sqrt{10}$ for 10-day VAR, $\sqrt{252}$ for 1-year VAR).

3.2 Applications of VAR:
• Setting maximum risk limits for trading desks and treasury dealers.
• Capital adequacy and asset-liability management (ALM) in commercial banks.
• Performance measurement: Risk-adjusted return on capital (RAROC).
• Formulating corporate hedging strategies against commodity, forex, and equity exposure.

4. METHODS FOR IDENTIFICATION AND MANAGEMENT OF FINANCIAL RISKS
================================================================================

4.1 Counterparty Risk:
Early Warning Signs / Identification:
• Failure to secure necessary working capital or resources to execute contracts.
• Regulatory penalties or license suspensions by governmental authorities.
• Adverse foreign government actions or international sanctions.
• Default notices and deteriorating credit ratings.
Risk Management Techniques:
1. Comprehensive pre-transaction due diligence.
2. Single-borrower and connected-party exposure caps.
3. Continuous credit monitoring and periodic internal rating reviews.
4. Prompt legal intervention and margin calls upon early default.
5. Requiring bank guarantees, letters of credit, collateral, or credit insurance.

4.2 Political Risk:
Identification Indicators:
• Host country mandates for compulsory local equity participation or local employment.
• Currency exchange controls and capital repatriation freezes.
• Selective taxation, product price caps, or nationalization threats.
Assessment Tools:
• International political risk indices published by reputable intelligence agencies.
• Macroeconomic stability indicators (foreign exchange reserves, fiscal deficits).
• Evaluation of ruling regime stability and public sentiment.
• Diplomatic briefings from embassies in host nations.
Mitigation Strategies:
1. Sourcing raw materials, supplies, and labour locally.
2. Entering into joint ventures with prominent domestic partners in the host country.
3. Securing local debt financing (creates a natural hedge against expropriation).
4. Negotiating bilateral investment treaties (BITs) and sovereign guarantees prior to capital deployment.

4.3 Interest Rate Risk:
Identification Parameters:
• Central bank monetary policy announcements (Repo rate, CRR, reverse repo changes).
• Government macro actions (market borrowing programs, demonetization, fiscal policy).
• Industrial production indices (IIP), GDP growth trajectories, and inflation data.
• Foreign Institutional Investor (FII) capital inflows and outflows.
Mitigation: Interest rate swaps (IRS), Forward Rate Agreements (FRAs), Interest Rate Futures (IRF), and Collar strategies.

4.4 Currency Risk:
Identification Parameters:
• Geopolitical shocks and governmental decisions (e.g., Brexit, trade embargoes).
• Nominal interest rate differentials (Interest Rate Parity - IRP).
• Differential inflation rates between trading partners (Purchasing Power Parity - PPP).
• War, regional conflicts, trade balance deficits, and global commodity shocks.
Mitigation: Forward contracts, currency futures, options, Non-Deliverable Forwards (NDF), and currency swaps.
